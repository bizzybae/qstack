Where the energy comes from

Four confirmed atmospheric energy sources

Thermals High

Sunlight heats the ground unevenly. Warm patches launch buoyant columns of rising air that fill the lowest 1 to 3 km of the atmosphere on convective days. Measured core updrafts run 1 to 5 m/s routinely, and 8 to 10 m/s in strong desert conditions. Thermals recur every few kilometers for six to eight hours on a good day and are often capped by cumulus clouds, which act as visible markers the aircraft's sky camera can track.

Ridge lift High

Wind hitting a slope deflects upward. To first order the vertical component is the wind speed times the sine of the slope angle, so a 10 m/s breeze on a moderate ridge yields 2 to 4 m/s of lift along the whole upwind face, day or night, in any season. Because it is anchored to terrain, ridge lift can be mapped once and exploited forever. Ridge routes have carried crewed sailplanes more than 1,500 km in a day.

Mountain wave High

Stable air flowing over a mountain range sets up standing waves downwind with smooth lift of 3 to 10 m/s reaching far above the terrain. Wave lift carried the unpowered Perlan 2 research sailplane to roughly 76,000 ft, higher than airliners fly. Wave is forecastable from standard weather model output.

Wind gradients High

Wind speed changes with height. Crossing that shear layer with the right repeating geometry extracts kinetic energy from the gradient itself, the trick albatrosses use to cross oceans while barely flapping. Radio-controlled gliders using the same physics have exceeded 850 km/h with zero propulsion Modeled on the exact record figure; the phenomenon itself is textbook. AENS treats dynamic soaring as a later-phase capability for over-water missions.

The math, end to end

Reference aircraft: every number on this site, derived

The open reference design, AENS-R1, is a 25 kg electric sailplane-class drone: 5.2 m span, best glide ratio 30:1 at 65 km/h, minimum sink 0.6 m/s, a 2.0 kg battery pack storing 400 Wh, and a combined motor, controller, and propeller climb efficiency of 65 percent.

1. What level flight costs High

P_air = m · g · v_sink = 25 kg · 9.81 m/s² · 0.6 m/s = 147 W
P_battery = P_air / η = 147 / 0.65 ≈ 226 W
Endurance, motor always on = 400 Wh / 226 W ≈ 1.8 hours

That 1.8 hours is the honest baseline: what this aircraft does with no atmospheric help, all day, every day.

2. What one thermal is worth High

Climb rate in a 2.0 m/s thermal = 2.0 − 0.6 = 1.4 m/s → 1,000 m in about 12 minutes
Energy banked = m · g · h = 25 · 9.81 · 1,000 = 245 kJ = 68 Wh mechanical
Battery equivalent = 68 / 0.65 ≈ 105 Wh = 26% of the pack, per climb
Glide from that altitude = 1,000 m · 30 = 30 km of forward travel, free

On an ordinary convective afternoon the aircraft can repeat this several times per hour. Note what is not being claimed: no perpetual motion, no violation of anything. The energy is solar. The atmosphere is the delivery system. The aircraft is just finally carrying a receiver.

3. What that does to endurance Modeled

Motor-off fraction f in moderate convection ≈ 0.5 to 0.7 (matches prior government flight campaigns)
Average draw = (1 − f) · 226 W ≈ 68 to 113 W → endurance ≈ 3.5 to 5.9 hours, a 2x to 3x multiplier
All-weather floor, no thermals used: optimal speed-to-fly and wind routing ≈ 5 to 15% savings

The multiplier is the modeled quantity, scheduled as the first flight-test milestone. The individual ingredients, updraft strength, sink rate, and mgh, are all high confidence.

System architecture

Four layers, no research breakthroughs required

L1 / Sensing and estimation

The aircraft cannot see the wind, but it can feel it. Inertial velocity from GNSS/INS minus air-relative velocity from a multi-hole probe equals the 3D wind vector at the aircraft's position. Subtract the aircraft's known sink rate and what remains is the vertical motion of the air itself. This inversion is standard flight mechanics, flown in research systems for two decades.

L2 / The probabilistic energy map

The surrounding sky is divided into cells, roughly 100 to 500 m across, each holding an estimate of lift, wind, and turbulence plus an uncertainty. Three streams feed it: the aircraft's own precise measurements, camera observations of hot ground and cumulus, and priors from weather models, terrain physics, and the fleet's history. The output is calibrated probability, exactly what a rational planner needs.

L3 / The energy-optimal planner

A receding-horizon optimizer chooses routes that minimize battery use subject to arrival time, terrain, airspace, turbulence limits, and one inviolable rule: the reserve needed to finish the mission with zero atmospheric help is never spent. It generalizes MacCready speed-to-fly theory, the 1940s optimal-control result that still wins gliding championships, from a scalar rule into a full spatial field optimization. Learned models propose; a deterministic, auditable safety layer disposes.

L4 / The fleet learning network

Every flight uploads anonymized observations: where lift was found, how strong, at what altitude and hour. Within an operating area, one aircraft's discovery routes the next aircraft within minutes. Over seasons the fleet builds an energy climatology: the south slope that triggers at 1 pm, the ridge that works in any northwest wind. Algorithms can be copied. Years of flown data cannot.

Prior flight demonstrations

The receipts High

05
NASA Dryden, 2005 to 2007. An autonomous motorized sailplane detected thermals from onboard energy-state estimates and centered them without human help, gaining meaningful altitude per encounter.
15
US Naval Research Laboratory, ALOFT. Multi-hour small drone flights with the propulsion system off for the majority of flight time, plus cooperative soaring in which two aircraft shared thermal locations, the embryo of the fleet map.
18
Nature, 2018. A reinforcement learning policy trained in real atmospheric turbulence soared a glider in the field. Microsoft Research flew AI-piloted sailplanes with Bayesian planning the same year.

The three gaps every prior effort left open

1. No persistent shared map. Each research flight started ignorant. The compounding dataset was never built.

2. No fusion with weather models. Kilometer-scale forecasts of convective strength already exist and were ignored as priors.

3. No product. No retrofit kits, no operator interfaces, no certification-conscious software architecture, no business model. The science is done. The productization is the opportunity.

Atmospheric energy is intermittent: weak at night, over cold water, and under overcast. That is why the first markets are daytime overland missions, why the planner treats free energy as a bonus rather than a budget line, and why the system degrades gracefully to a normal electric aircraft with better routing, never below it.
