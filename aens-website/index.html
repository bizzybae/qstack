The problem

Everyone in electric aviation is fighting the same battery

A kilogram of jet fuel carries roughly 20 times more useful energy than a kilogram of the best certified battery pack, even after engine losses. That single number is why battery drones fly for under an hour while fuel aircraft fly all day.

Batteries improve about 5 to 8 percent per year. Hundreds of companies are waiting for that curve, and whenever it moves, it moves for all of them at once. Waiting is not an edge.

Endurance = energy stored ÷ power required. The whole industry works on the top of that fraction. AENS works on the bottom, and in rising air it drives the power required below zero.

The idea, in one minute

Hawks do not carry fuel. Neither should software have to.

Watch a hawk on a summer afternoon. It finds a column of warm rising air, circles inside it, climbs hundreds of meters without a single flap, then glides for kilometers. Glider pilots copy the trick and fly across entire countries on it. AENS gives that instinct to any electric aircraft.

01 / See the invisible

A small sensor kit measures how the air is pushing the aircraft around, thousands of times a second. An infrared camera watches which ground is hot, and a sky camera watches the clouds that mark rising air. Together they reveal a live 3D picture of the wind that no human eye can see.

02 / Navigate in 4D

Normal autopilots know latitude, longitude, and altitude, and ask "what is the shortest path?" AENS adds a fourth dimension, energy availability, and asks a better question: "which path needs the least battery?" Sometimes a 500 meter detour into rising air pays for 30 kilometers of free glide.

03 / Learn as a fleet

Every equipped aircraft reports where it found lift. One aircraft's discovery becomes every aircraft's route option, and over the seasons the fleet builds a permanent map of where the sky's energy lives. Think Waze, but for energy in the air instead of traffic on the road.

Hard safety rule: the battery reserve needed to finish the mission with zero help from the atmosphere is never touched. Free energy is always a bonus, never a dependency.

Physics that checks out

One climb in rising air is worth a quarter of the battery

These are not projections. They are conservation of energy, plus updraft speeds that meteorologists have measured for a century, plus glide performance that sailplane makers have delivered for decades.

Σ
The floor, any weather: flying the mathematically correct speeds through measured lift and sink saves an estimated 5 to 15 percent of energy before a single thermal is used.
↑
An ordinary sunny day: motor off for the majority of the flight, the regime government flight tests already demonstrated. Endurance class: 2x on the same battery.
∞
A strong day: quasi-indefinite daytime flight for a sailplane class aircraft, with the battery reserved for takeoff and landing.

Already flown

The hard part has been demonstrated. Nobody productized it.

NASA, 2005 to 2007

An autonomous drone found and climbed real thermals in flight tests over the Mojave, gaining altitude with the motor off.

US Naval Research Lab

The ALOFT program flew multi-hour small drone flights with the motor off for most of the flight, and even had two aircraft share thermal locations with each other.

Nature journal, 2018

Researchers trained a learning algorithm to soar a glider in real turbulent air, published in one of the world's top scientific journals. Microsoft Research flew AI sailplanes the same year.

Every one of those systems started each flight knowing nothing. None of them built the thing that compounds: the shared, permanent map of where the sky's energy lives. That map is the business.

The economics

Open source idea. Very closed loop on profit.

Why give the algorithms away?

The core soaring algorithms are already public science, published by NASA, the Navy, and universities. Pretending to own them would be a paper moat. Open sourcing the flight code does what open source does best: it recruits researchers, builds trust with defense buyers who must audit their autonomy, and makes AENS the standard everyone builds on.

Then where does the money come from?

The same places it comes from for Android and Red Hat. The code is free. Three things are not:

K
Certified sensor kits. Calibrated, flight-proven hardware sold at roughly $24k per aircraft. Anyone can read the schematic; operators still buy the certified unit.
M
The energy map network. A hosted subscription, about $9k per aircraft per year, that gives every customer the fleet's accumulated knowledge of where lift lives. The data cannot be forked. It has to be flown.
S
Integration and support. Engineering contracts to put AENS on manufacturers' aircraft, plus defense programs.
